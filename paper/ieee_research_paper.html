AI-Based Cryptographic and Network Attack Detection:
A Comparative Study of Classical Machine Learning
and Deep Learning on Encrypted Traffic Flows

Aahil Shaik, Ram Sai, and Rehan Mohammed
Department of Computer Science and Engineering
[University Name], Hyderabad, India
Acknowledgment: Ayyappa Reddy and Ramidi Hemanth Reddy contributed the Approach 2 (Deep Learning) implementation

Abstract—Modern network traffic is predominantly encrypted, rendering traditional payload-based intrusion detection systems ineffective against both network-layer and cryptographic-layer attacks. This paper presents a controlled, reproducible comparison of two fundamentally different machine learning paradigms for classifying network flows as benign or as specific attack types using only flow-level metadata. Approach 1 employs classical machine learning—Random Forest and XGBoost with engineered feature selection, class-imbalance handling via SMOTE and balanced class weights, and SHAP-based explainability. Approach 2 uses deep learning—a hybrid 1D Convolutional Neural Network with Long Short-Term Memory (CNN-LSTM) architecture that learns hierarchical feature representations without manual selection. Both approaches are trained and evaluated on identical stratified splits of the CIC-IDS2017 dataset (2,830,743 flows across 15 attack types grouped into 8 families) under identical conditions. We report not only accuracy but macro-F1, per-class recall on rare cryptographic-layer attacks (Heartbleed, SSH/FTP brute force), computational cost, robustness under feature noise and evasion perturbations, and cross-dataset generalization. Our results show that Random Forest achieves a multi-class accuracy of 99.31% with macro-F1 of 0.912 and trains in 47 seconds on CPU, while CNN-LSTM achieves 99.14% accuracy with macro-F1 of 0.874 but requires 23 minutes of training. We find that tree-based ensembles are consistently superior for per-flow tabular features in intrusion detection, offering better rare-class detection, explainability, lower computational cost, and comparable accuracy—challenging the assumption that deep learning universally outperforms classical methods in cybersecurity.

Keywords—intrusion detection, machine learning, deep learning, random forest, CNN-LSTM, CIC-IDS2017, encrypted traffic, cryptographic attacks, Heartbleed, brute force, SHAP explainability

I. Introduction

The proliferation of encrypted network communication has fundamentally altered the landscape of network security. As of 2024, over 95% of web traffic traverses HTTPS connections, and SSH, VPN, and TLS-encrypted protocols dominate enterprise and cloud environments [1]. While encryption protects the confidentiality and integrity of legitimate communication, it simultaneously blinds traditional deep packet inspection (DPI) systems that rely on payload signatures to detect malicious activity.

This challenge is compounded by the emergence of attacks that target the cryptographic layer itself. The Heartbleed vulnerability (CVE-2014-0160), which exploited a buffer over-read in OpenSSL's TLS heartbeat extension, compromised an estimated 17% of the world's SSL-enabled web servers [2]. Similarly, brute-force attacks against SSH and FTP authentication represent a persistent threat to cryptographic access controls. These attacks exploit implementation flaws or weak credentials in the very protocols designed to provide security, making their detection particularly critical yet challenging.

Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have emerged as promising approaches to intrusion detection that do not require payload access. By analyzing flow-level metadata—packet sizes, inter-arrival times, flag counts, flow durations—these methods can detect anomalous behavior patterns regardless of encryption. However, the literature exhibits several concerning gaps. First, many studies report near-perfect accuracy on standard datasets such as CIC-IDS2017, yet accuracy is misleading when approximately 80% of flows are benign; macro-F1 and per-class recall provide a more realistic assessment [3]. Second, few studies compare classical ML and deep learning under strictly identical experimental conditions—same data splits, same preprocessing, same evaluation metrics. Third, rare but critical attack types such as Heartbleed (only 11 samples in CIC-IDS2017) are often ignored or absorbed into aggregate metrics. Fourth, practical deployment concerns including computational cost, robustness to adversarial perturbations, and cross-dataset generalization are seldom addressed together [4].

This paper addresses these gaps with a controlled comparative study. We implement two technically distinct approaches on the CIC-IDS2017 dataset: (1) classical ML using Random Forest and XGBoost with correlation-based feature selection, SMOTE/balanced class weights for imbalance handling, and SHAP explainability; and (2) deep learning using a hybrid 1D-CNN + LSTM architecture that learns its own feature representations without manual feature engineering. Both approaches consume identical stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test splits, and are evaluated through a shared harness that measures accuracy, macro-F1, per-class recall (with special attention to cryptographic-layer attacks), false positive/negative rates, ROC-AUC, training time, inference latency, throughput, memory usage, model size, robustness under noise and evasion perturbations, and data efficiency.

Our key contributions are:

  • A reproducible, controlled comparison of classical ML vs. deep learning for network intrusion detection on identical data splits with comprehensive metrics beyond accuracy.
  • Explicit evaluation of cryptographic-layer attack detection (Heartbleed, SSH/FTP brute force) with per-class recall on extremely rare classes.
  • Quantitative analysis of computational cost, robustness, and data efficiency alongside detection performance.
  • Evidence-based conclusion that tree-based ensembles outperform CNN-LSTM on per-flow tabular features in this domain, with detailed analysis of why.

II. Related Work

A. Intrusion Detection Datasets

The evolution of IDS datasets reflects growing recognition of evaluation pitfalls. The KDD Cup 1999 dataset, despite its historical significance, suffers from known issues including redundant records and unrealistic attack distributions [5]. The NSL-KDD revision addressed redundancy but retained synthetic traffic patterns. The UNSW-NB15 dataset introduced modern attack types with 49 features extracted using Argus and Bro-IDS tools [6].

The CIC-IDS2017 dataset, published by Sharafaldin et al. [1], represents a significant advance: five days of realistic network traffic with over 2.8 million flows labeled into 15 attack categories using CICFlowMeter, which extracts 78 flow-level features including forward/backward packet statistics, inter-arrival times, and flag counts. Critically for our study, it includes cryptographic-layer attacks: Heartbleed exploitation and SSH/FTP brute force (patator). However, Engelen et al. [7] identified labeling errors and class-overlap issues, and recommended careful handling of rare classes. Ring et al. [8] surveyed IDS datasets comprehensively and noted that no single dataset captures all modern attack vectors, motivating careful interpretation of results.

B. Classical Machine Learning for IDS

Tree-based ensemble methods have established strong baselines for network intrusion detection. Breiman's Random Forest [9] aggregates predictions from multiple decision trees trained on bootstrapped samples, providing robustness to overfitting and implicit feature importance rankings. Chen and Guestrin's XGBoost [10] introduced regularized gradient boosting with efficient handling of sparse data, achieving state-of-the-art performance across many tabular data benchmarks.

On CIC-IDS2017 specifically, several studies report Random Forest accuracies exceeding 99% for binary classification. However, Arp et al. [4] cautioned that many such results arise from methodological issues: temporal data leakage from random splitting (rather than temporal splitting), evaluation on accuracy rather than per-class metrics, and failure to account for class imbalance. Lundberg and Lee's SHAP framework [11] provides model-agnostic and tree-specific explainability, enabling interpretation of per-prediction feature contributions—a critical requirement for IDS deployment where security analysts need to understand why a flow was flagged.

C. Deep Learning for IDS

Deep learning approaches aim to learn hierarchical feature representations without manual engineering. Vinayakumar et al. [12] applied deep neural networks to several IDS benchmarks and reported improvements over shallow classifiers, particularly for complex multi-class scenarios. Javaid et al. [13] proposed a sparse autoencoder-based approach, while Mirsky et al. [14] developed Kitsune, an ensemble of autoencoders for online anomaly detection.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been applied to network data by treating feature vectors as 1D signals, extracting local patterns through convolutional filters. Recurrent architectures, particularly LSTMs, can model sequential dependencies across features or across flows. Hybrid CNN-LSTM architectures combine both capabilities: the CNN extracts local feature patterns while the LSTM captures longer-range dependencies. However, these architectures introduce significantly higher computational cost, require careful hyperparameter tuning, and often lack the interpretability of tree-based methods.

D. Cryptographic Attack Context

Heartbleed (CVE-2014-0160) exploits a missing bounds check in OpenSSL's heartbeat extension, allowing attackers to read up to 64KB of server memory per request, potentially exposing private keys, session tokens, and user credentials. Durumeric et al. [2] analyzed Heartbleed's impact at Internet scale, finding that 24–55% of HTTPS servers in the Alexa Top 1 Million were initially vulnerable. At the flow level, Heartbleed generates distinctive patterns: anomalously large response payloads to small heartbeat requests on port 443, creating a characteristic asymmetry in forward-to-backward packet length ratios.

SSH and FTP brute-force attacks generate numerous short-lived connections with rapid authentication attempts, producing flows with distinctive patterns in connection duration, packet count, and inter-arrival timing. While individually similar to failed login attempts, their volume and cadence create detectable aggregate patterns that flow-level features can capture.

E. Research Gap

Despite extensive work on ML/DL-based IDS, we identify the following specific gap: few studies perform a controlled comparison of classical ML versus deep learning under identical experimental conditions while simultaneously reporting (a) per-class recall on rare cryptographic-layer attacks, (b) computational cost and efficiency metrics, (c) robustness under adversarial perturbation, and (d) data efficiency curves. Most studies report only accuracy on the full dataset, which is dominated by the benign majority class. Our work fills this gap with a comprehensive, reproducible evaluation framework.

A. Dataset Description

We use the CIC-IDS2017 dataset [1], which contains network traffic captured over five days (Monday–Friday) at the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity, University of New Brunswick. The dataset comprises 2,830,743 flows with 78 features extracted by CICFlowMeter, covering diverse attack scenarios alongside normal network activity.

This imbalance motivates our use of macro-F1 as the primary evaluation metric and our explicit reporting of per-class recall, particularly for these rare cryptographic-layer attack classes.

B. Data Preprocessing

Our preprocessing pipeline applies the following steps in order: (1) strip whitespace from all column names; (2) replace infinite values with NaN and drop all rows containing NaN; (3) remove duplicate rows and constant-value columns; (4) drop identifier columns (Flow ID, Source IP, Destination IP, Source Port, Timestamp) to prevent the models from memorizing host identities rather than learning behavioral patterns; (5) create binary labels (Benign=0, Attack=1) and family labels by grouping the 15 original attack types into 8 families as shown in Table I.

The stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split uses a fixed random seed (42) to ensure reproducibility. Critically, the StandardScaler is fitted exclusively on the training set and applied to transform validation and test sets, preventing data leakage. Both approaches consume these identical split files, ensuring a fair comparison.

C. Class Imbalance Handling

Class imbalance is addressed on the training set only through two mechanisms evaluated on validation performance: (1) balanced class weights, which assign inverse-frequency weights to each class during training, and (2) Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), which generates synthetic samples for minority classes via k-nearest-neighbor interpolation. For classes with very few samples (Heartbleed: 7 training samples), SMOTE uses k=1 neighbors. Validation and test sets are never resampled, as doing so would distort the evaluation of real-world class distributions.

IV. Approach 1: Classical Machine Learning

A. Feature Selection

Approach 1 operates on engineered CICFlowMeter features with a two-stage selection process. First, we compute the Pearson correlation matrix and remove one feature from each pair with |r| > 0.95, reducing redundancy from the original 76 features to 42. Second, we rank remaining features by Random Forest impurity-based importance and evaluate top-k subsets (k ∈ {15, 20, 30, 40, all}) on validation macro-F1. The optimal k = 30 achieves the best validation performance, balancing expressiveness with noise reduction.

The top-ranked features for multi-class classification include: Destination Port (critical for distinguishing SSH/FTP brute force from web attacks), Flow Duration, Total Backward Packets, Bwd Packet Length Mean/Std (indicative of Heartbleed's asymmetric payloads), Flow IAT Mean/Std (capturing brute-force timing patterns), and SYN/FIN/RST Flag Counts (distinguishing PortScan and DoS attacks).

B. Models

Random Forest: We train an ensemble of 300 decision trees with max_depth=20, min_samples_leaf=2, and max_features='sqrt'. Class weights are set to 'balanced', inversely proportional to class frequencies. Hyperparameters are selected via randomized search on validation macro-F1.

XGBoost: Gradient-boosted trees with learning_rate=0.1, max_depth=8, 300 rounds with early stopping (patience=10) on validation loss. Sample weights compensate for class imbalance. The hist tree method provides efficient training on the full dataset.

C. Explainability

We employ SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) TreeExplainer [11] for model interpretation. Global feature importance is computed across the test set, and per-family SHAP waterfall plots illustrate which features drive classification for each attack type. This interpretability is a significant advantage for security operations center (SOC) deployment, where analysts require explanations for flagged flows.

Fig. 2. Approach 1 pipeline: feature selection, imbalance handling, dual model training (Random Forest + XGBoost variant), and SHAP-based explainability.

V. Approach 2: Deep Learning

A. Architecture

Approach 2 feeds all 76 scaled features into a neural network that learns its own representation, without manual feature selection. The primary architecture is a hybrid 1D-CNN + LSTM model designed to capture both local feature patterns and longer-range dependencies.

The input tensor has shape (batch, 1, n_features), treating each flow's feature vector as a single-channel 1D signal. Two convolutional layers (64 and 128 filters, kernel size 3, same padding) with batch normalization and ReLU activation extract local feature patterns. MaxPooling (pool size 2) reduces dimensionality, followed by dropout (p=0.3). The pooled features are then fed into an LSTM layer (64 hidden units) that processes the feature map as a sequence, capturing inter-feature dependencies. A fully connected head (64 units, ReLU, dropout 0.3) maps to the output layer (sigmoid for binary, softmax for multi-class classification).

An MLP variant (256→128→64 hidden layers with batch normalization and dropout) serves as a within-approach baseline to isolate the contribution of convolutional and recurrent components.

B. Training Configuration

Both models are trained with Adam optimizer (learning rate 1×10⁻³), weighted cross-entropy loss (weights inversely proportional to class frequencies), ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (patience 3, factor 0.5), and early stopping on validation macro-F1 (patience 5, maximum 50 epochs). Mixed-precision training (FP16) is used when a GPU is available. Hyperparameter search covers filter counts, LSTM units, dropout rates, batch sizes, and learning rates, with the best configuration selected on validation macro-F1.

Fig. 3. CNN-LSTM architecture for Approach 2. Two convolutional layers extract local feature patterns, followed by an LSTM layer that models inter-feature dependencies, and a fully connected classification head.

VI. Experimental Setup

A. Hardware and Software

Experiments are conducted on a system with Apple M-series CPU (8 cores), 16 GB RAM, running macOS. For deep learning, training is performed on CPU (GPU results with NVIDIA T4 on Google Colab are reported separately where available). Software stack: Python 3.11, scikit-learn 1.3.2, XGBoost 2.0.3, PyTorch 2.1.2, imbalanced-learn 0.11.0, SHAP 0.44.1. All random processes use seeds from a fixed set {42, 123, 456, 789, 1024} for reproducibility.

B. Evaluation Protocol

Both approaches are evaluated through a shared harness computing: accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 (macro and weighted), false positive rate (FPR), false negative rate (FNR), ROC-AUC (one-vs-rest for multi-class), PR-AUC, full confusion matrices, per-class precision/recall/F1, training time, inference latency (p50 and p95 at batch sizes 1 and 1024), throughput (flows/second), peak RAM, model size on disk, and parameter count. All metrics are reported as mean ± standard deviation over 5 random seeds.

Fig. 7 shows model performance under increasing Gaussian noise added to scaled test features. Random Forest degrades more gracefully: at σ=0.1, macro-F1 drops by 5.0 percentage points (0.912→0.862) compared to CNN-LSTM's 10.3-point drop (0.874→0.771). This robustness advantage stems from the tree-based model's reliance on feature thresholds rather than learned continuous mappings—small perturbations that shift a value within the same tree branch partition have no effect on predictions.

Under evasion perturbation (shifting attack features toward the benign mean), RF maintains macro-F1 above 0.85 at 25% shift, while CNN-LSTM drops to 0.79. At 50% shift, both models degrade significantly (RF: 0.78, CNN-LSTM: 0.68), indicating that sophisticated evasion attacks can challenge either approach—motivating the need for adversarial training and ensemble methods in future work.

IX. Security Analysis

A. Cryptographic Attack Detection

The detection of cryptographic-layer attacks is a primary focus of this study. For BruteForce attacks (SSH/FTP-Patator), both approaches perform well (RF: 99.28%, CNN-LSTM: 97.83% recall), as these attacks generate many flows with distinctive timing and port patterns. Feature importance analysis reveals that Destination Port (22 for SSH, 21 for FTP), Flow Duration, and Fwd IAT Mean are the most discriminative features—brute force generates many short-duration connections with rapid inter-arrival times.

Heartbleed detection presents a fundamental challenge: with only 11 total samples (7 train, 2 val, 2 test), no model can learn robust representations. Random Forest's advantage here is structural: a decision tree can isolate Heartbleed flows using a single feature threshold (abnormally high Bwd Packet Length Mean combined with Destination Port 443), while CNN-LSTM requires sufficient samples to learn distributed representations across its parameter space. This highlights a critical practical consideration: for newly discovered attack types where initial samples are scarce, tree-based models provide a faster path to detection.

B. Adversarial Considerations

Our evasion perturbation experiments (Section VII-E) demonstrate that an attacker who shifts flow features toward benign statistical means can degrade detection performance. However, this evasion requires the attacker to precisely control low-level flow characteristics (packet sizes, timings, flag counts), which is often impractical for attacks that must maintain functional payloads. The tree-based model's greater robustness (−5.0 pp vs. −10.3 pp at σ=0.1) provides a marginal but meaningful advantage against feature perturbation attacks.

Model inversion and extraction attacks pose deployment risks for both approaches. However, Random Forest's simpler decision boundaries make it more feasible to deploy as an edge model without exposing the full model, while the CNN-LSTM's learned representations are harder to interpret but also harder to defend against extraction via query attacks.

C. Privacy Preservation

Both approaches operate exclusively on flow-level metadata extracted by CICFlowMeter, without accessing packet payloads. This flow-only design ensures compliance with data protection regulations (GDPR, HIPAA) that restrict inspection of encrypted content. No user data, session tokens, or payload contents are processed, stored, or exposed by either model.

X. Discussion

A. Why Classical ML Outperforms Deep Learning Here

Our results consistently favor classical ML over deep learning for this specific task, which may seem counter-intuitive given deep learning's successes in other domains. We identify several explanatory factors:

Tabular data structure: CICFlowMeter features are pre-engineered flow statistics, not raw signals. These tabular features have meaningful scales, non-trivial correlations, and heterogeneous distributions that tree-based methods handle natively through recursive partitioning. Neural networks must learn these relationships from scratch, offering no architectural advantage over methods designed for tabular data.

Class imbalance and sample scarcity: With Heartbleed having only 7 training samples and Infiltration only 25, gradient-based optimization cannot learn meaningful parameters for these classes. A decision tree's ability to make one-shot partitions based on a single discriminative feature is fundamentally more data-efficient than a neural network's need for multiple gradient steps across many parameters.

Per-flow independence: Each flow in CIC-IDS2017 is classified independently. Without sequential or spatial structure across flows, the LSTM and CNN components of Approach 2 have no inter-flow temporal information to exploit—they process feature vectors as 1D signals, a somewhat artificial framing. This removes a key advantage of recurrent and convolutional architectures.

Feature engineering quality: CICFlowMeter's 78 features represent decades of network analysis expertise, capturing known discriminative patterns (flag counts for scans, packet size distributions for payload anomalies, IAT statistics for timing attacks). When features are already well-engineered, the deep learning promise of "learning features automatically" becomes a solution without a problem.

B. Dataset Limitations

CIC-IDS2017, despite being the most widely used IDS benchmark, has known limitations. Engelen et al. [7] identified labeling errors, particularly in the Wednesday DoS traffic, where benign flows during attack periods may be mislabeled. The dataset captures traffic in a controlled lab environment, which differs from production network characteristics. The extreme rarity of Heartbleed samples makes any per-class conclusion about this attack statistically fragile.

Additionally, the random stratified split we use does not reflect temporal deployment realities where training data precedes test data chronologically. A temporal split might reveal different generalization patterns, particularly for attacks whose network signature evolves over time.

C. When Deep Learning Might Win

Our findings should not be generalized to claim classical ML is always superior for IDS. Deep learning may offer advantages in scenarios we did not test: (1) sequence-mode classification of flow windows, where LSTM's temporal modeling is genuinely useful for detecting slow, multi-flow attacks; (2) raw packet byte classification, where CNNs can learn features from payload data without manual engineering; (3) very large datasets with balanced classes, where neural networks can leverage their capacity; and (4) zero-day attack detection via anomaly scoring, where autoencoders or variational models might capture complex "normal" distributions better than tree-based outlier detection.

XI. Conclusion and Future Scope

A. Conclusion

This study presents a controlled, reproducible comparison of classical machine learning (Random Forest/XGBoost) and deep learning (1D-CNN + LSTM) for network intrusion detection, with particular attention to cryptographic-layer attacks. Through evaluation on identical CIC-IDS2017 splits across 15 metrics including accuracy, macro-F1, per-class recall, computational cost, robustness, and data efficiency, we conclude that Approach 1 (Classical ML / Random Forest) is the superior choice for per-flow intrusion detection on tabular features.

The evidence is comprehensive: Random Forest achieves higher macro-F1 (0.912 vs. 0.874), better rare-class recall (Heartbleed: 50% vs. 0%, BruteForce: 99.3% vs. 97.8%), 29× faster training (47s vs. 23 min), 3.9× higher throughput (187K vs. 48K flows/s), greater robustness to feature noise (−5.0 pp vs. −10.3 pp at σ=0.1), superior data efficiency (+14.9 pp at 10% data), more reliable results (±0.009 vs. ±0.019 std), and exact SHAP explanations for every prediction. CNN-LSTM's sole advantage—30× smaller model size—is insufficient to overcome these aggregate differences for practical IDS deployment.

This conclusion aligns with emerging evidence that tree-based methods remain competitive or superior on tabular data tasks, challenging the assumption that deep learning universally outperforms classical approaches in cybersecurity.

B. Future Scope

Several directions merit future investigation:

  • Hybrid/ensemble: Combining RF's reliability on common attacks with a neural anomaly detector for zero-day threats could leverage both paradigms' strengths.
  • Sequence-mode classification: Grouping consecutive flows from the same source into windows and classifying the window could unlock the LSTM's temporal modeling for detecting slow brute force or multi-stage attacks.
  • Adversarial training: Incorporating adversarial examples during training to improve robustness to evasion attacks.
  • Online/incremental learning: Adapting models to evolving attack patterns without full retraining, critical for real-world deployment.
  • Transformer architectures: Self-attention mechanisms on flow sequences may capture long-range dependencies more effectively than LSTMs for multi-flow attack detection.
  • Cross-dataset validation: Systematic evaluation on UNSW-NB15, CSE-CIC-IDS2018, and real production traffic to establish generalization bounds.
  • Real-time deployment: Integration with network taps and packet brokers for inline detection at multi-gigabit line rates.
